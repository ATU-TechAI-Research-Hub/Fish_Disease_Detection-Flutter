# Water quality and fish health

Water quality must be interpreted for the cultured species, life stage,
salinity, stocking density, feed load, and production system. Measure trends
with calibrated instruments; a single reading is rarely enough.

## Dissolved oxygen

Low dissolved oxygen can cause surface piping or gasping, crowding near inflows,
reduced feeding, lethargy, and sudden early-morning mortality. Warm water holds
less oxygen. Algal respiration, decomposition, overfeeding, and high biomass
can drive oxygen down overnight. As a broad operational target, many warm-water
finfish systems aim to remain above about 5 mg/L, but species-specific limits
must take priority. Emergency actions include stopping feed temporarily,
increasing aeration, improving circulation, and checking for dead algae or
organic loading. Avoid abrupt water changes that also cause temperature or pH
shock.

## Ammonia and nitrite

Total ammonia nitrogen contains ionized ammonium and more toxic un-ionized
ammonia. The toxic fraction rises as pH and temperature rise. Any detectable
un-ionized ammonia deserves investigation. Common contributors are excessive
feed, decaying material, high stocking density, insufficient biofiltration, and
new or disrupted filters. Reduce the source, verify the test method, support
biofiltration, and make controlled water exchanges when appropriate.

Nitrite interferes with oxygen transport and may cause brown-blood disease,
respiratory distress, or unexplained weakness. Check biofilter function and
system-specific chloride management with a qualified aquaculture professional.

## pH, alkalinity, and temperature

Rapid change is often more harmful than a stable value near the edge of a
species' preferred range. Photosynthesis can create a daily pH cycle: lowest
near dawn and highest late in the day. Low alkalinity allows larger pH swings.
Record pH at consistent morning and afternoon times before correcting it.

Temperature controls metabolism, oxygen demand, pathogen growth, feeding, and
the toxicity of ammonia. Measure at the depth occupied by the fish. Make
temperature changes gradually and use ranges established for the farmed
species.

## Practical investigation sequence

1. Observe behavior before disturbing the fish.
2. Measure dissolved oxygen, temperature, pH, ammonia, and nitrite.
3. Review recent feed, stocking, weather, chemical, and water-source changes.
4. Inspect several fish, including newly affected and apparently normal fish.
5. Record photographs and mortalities.
6. Isolate affected stock when feasible and contact an aquatic animal-health
   professional when mortality is increasing or diagnosis is uncertain.
