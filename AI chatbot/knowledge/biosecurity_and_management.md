# Aquaculture biosecurity and preventive management

Biosecurity reduces the probability that pathogens enter, spread within, or
leave a farm. It works best as a written routine rather than an emergency-only
activity.

## Entry prevention

- Obtain stock from traceable suppliers with relevant health records.
- Quarantine new or returning fish in a physically separate system.
- Do not share wet nets, graders, buckets, boots, or transport water between
  quarantine and production units without cleaning and disinfection.
- Control visitors, vehicles, wildlife access, and unverified water sources.
- Use species- and region-appropriate vaccination where licensed and advised.

## Limiting spread

- Work from youngest or healthiest groups toward suspect groups.
- Assign equipment to each tank or pond where possible.
- Remove mortalities promptly, record them, and dispose of them lawfully.
- Separate clinically affected fish when this can be done without causing
  additional stress.
- Clean organic material before applying a suitable disinfectant; dirt and
  biofilm can reduce disinfectant effectiveness.
- Respect disinfectant concentration, contact time, material compatibility,
  rinsing, and worker-safety instructions.

## Feeding and stress

Overfeeding degrades water quality and fuels bacterial growth. Underfeeding or
poorly stored feed can impair condition and immunity. Use appropriate feed,
store it dry and protected from pests, monitor appetite, and adjust ration to
temperature, biomass, and observed consumption.

Crowding, rough handling, transport, poor oxygen, rapid temperature change, and
aggression can damage skin and gills and increase susceptibility to infection.
Plan handling to minimize duration and maintain oxygen.

## Records and outbreak response

Record stocking source, movement, feed, water quality, treatments, mortalities,
and photographs. During an outbreak:

1. Stabilize oxygen and other immediate environmental hazards.
2. Stop moving fish, water, and equipment between units.
3. Collect representative history, water measurements, and fresh specimens.
4. Seek laboratory or aquatic-veterinary confirmation before medication.
5. Follow legal prescription, residue, withdrawal, and discharge requirements.

Antibiotics do not treat viral, fungal, or parasitic disease and should not be
used simply because a lesion appears red. Culture and susceptibility testing
can help select an effective antibiotic for a confirmed bacterial infection.
Incorrect use can fail treatment, harm biofilters, leave residues, and promote
antimicrobial resistance.
