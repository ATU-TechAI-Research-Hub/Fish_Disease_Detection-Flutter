# Common fish-health questions and differential diagnoses

Visible signs are rarely specific to one disease. Confirm water quality and
farm history, examine multiple fish, and use microscopy, culture, histology, or
PCR where appropriate.

## White spots and Ich

Freshwater Ich, or white spot disease, is caused by the ciliate parasite
*Ichthyophthirius multifiliis*. Pinhead-sized white spots may occur on skin and
fins, with flashing, excess mucus, clamped fins, and respiratory distress when
gills are involved. Similar spots can result from other parasites, mucus,
debris, fungal material, or image reflections.

The parasite has host-attached and environmental life stages. Treatment is
stage-dependent and often requires repeated, system-wide management rather
than treating one visibly affected fish. Temperature changes alter the life
cycle but can also stress fish and reduce oxygen, so do not raise temperature
without confirming species tolerance and oxygen capacity. Seek locally
approved treatment guidance and protect biofilters.

## Fin rot and ulcers

"Fin rot" describes progressive fin erosion, not one definitive pathogen.
Predisposing factors include damaged fins, aggression, rough handling, crowding,
poor water quality, and chronic stress. Opportunistic bacteria may then invade.
Check ammonia, nitrite, dissolved oxygen, temperature, pH stability, stocking
density, and recent handling before choosing treatment.

Redness or ulcers can occur with Aeromonas and other bacterial infections, but
also with trauma, parasites, toxins, and water-quality injury. A photograph
cannot determine bacterial species or antibiotic susceptibility. Fresh
diagnostic samples should be collected before antimicrobial treatment.

## Cotton-like growth

Saprolegnia and related water molds can produce white-to-grey cotton-like
growth on skin, fins, eggs, or wounds. It is often secondary to injury, poor
condition, handling damage, or another infection. Mucus, bacterial colonies,
and debris can appear similar in photographs. Correct the predisposing stressor
and obtain diagnosis before applying chemicals because safe choices vary with
species, life stage, water chemistry, and regulations.

## Abnormal swimming or floating upside down

Loss of balance is a sign, not a diagnosis. Possible causes include:

- acute low oxygen or severe gill damage;
- temperature or pH shock;
- ammonia, nitrite, chlorine, or another toxic exposure;
- trauma or handling injury;
- buoyancy or swim-bladder dysfunction;
- gastrointestinal distension or inappropriate feeding;
- neurological, systemic bacterial, parasitic, or viral disease.

First observe whether several fish are affected. If many fish change behavior
at once, investigate oxygen, toxicants, temperature, and recent management
events urgently. Isolate a single affected fish only when this can be done
without additional stress. Do not puncture a swim bladder or medicate from a
photograph alone.

## Viral disease

Antibiotics do not treat viruses. Response typically emphasizes movement
control, biosecurity, supportive water quality, mortality records, and
laboratory confirmation. Some important aquatic animal diseases are reportable;
follow local veterinary and regulatory requirements.

## Medication stewardship

Salt, oxidizing agents, antiparasitics, antifungals, and antibiotics all have
system- and species-specific risks. Some treatments damage gills or
biofilters, become more toxic with particular water chemistry, or are not
licensed for food fish. Never combine products unless compatibility is known.
Use the product label and aquatic-veterinary direction, and observe legal
withdrawal periods. Culture and susceptibility testing should guide antibiotics
for confirmed bacterial disease; do not use antibiotics for prevention or for
an unconfirmed visual classification.
